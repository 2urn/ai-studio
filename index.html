Digital Broadsides · Thinking Tools No. 02

Before you invite
AI into the studio

A field guide to five ethical fault lines — IP, authorship, epistemic extraction,
environmental cost, and alignment

CHAMY·XYZ
Dimitry Saïd Chamy
CHAMY·XYZ
July 2026

AI has entered the studio faster than most artists' understanding of it. Millions use it daily — to generate images, draft statements, prototype forms — without a clear picture of what is happening underneath or what they are authorizing in the process.

That gap creates real problems. It naturalizes a system built on unconsented extraction. It encourages misplaced trust in outputs that are confidently wrong. It makes invisible a labor chain that extends from low-wage annotators in the Global South to the power grids of rural communities that never voted on the buildout.

This is not an argument against use. It is a set of tools for use with open eyes. The five fault lines laid out here — drawn from current litigation, technical research, and activist testimony — are the minimum ethical terrain any practitioner should walk before inviting these systems into the work.

Each section identifies a common misread, names the actual structure, and offers a reframe for the studio or classroom. The decision flows that follow are not prescriptive. They are a mirror.

"I truly felt like someone had backed up a truck to my imagination and stolen everything I'd ever created."

David Baldacci · Senate Judiciary Committee · July 2025

§ 1 — Five fault lines tap any to expand
Fault line 01
IP & Authorship
Concepts 2 · 8 · 14
Active litigation
Common misread

"The model learned from my work the way a human student does — by seeing it and being inspired."

Reframe

Training is statistical ingestion at industrial scale — billions of images without consent, credit, or the ability to forget. Karla Ortiz: "industrial-level identity theft." Style is not copyrightable; the extraction mechanism isn't inspiration.

US Supreme Court declined to hear AI copyright case, March 2026. ~60 active lawsuits globally.

Fault line 02
Epistemic & Cognitive Extraction
Concepts 1 · 5 · 10
Structurally underexamined
Common misread

"AI is a thinking partner — it understands what I mean and responds creatively."

Reframe

The model has no understanding, no intent, no inner life. What feels like dialogue is next-token prediction. The extraction runs one direction: human creative work compressed into weights that generate economic value for a few companies, returned to users as a product. Glissant's right to opacity applies: your creative process needn't be legible to a training pipeline.

Fault line 03
Hallucination as Feature
Concept 9
Misframed as fixable
Common misread

"Hallucinations are glitches — future models will fix this. For now I just fact-check."

Reframe

Karpathy: "Hallucination is all LLMs do." The model is doing the same thing when it gets it right as when it gets it wrong. Fluent output is not evidence of accuracy. For artists working with history, attribution, or research, this is consequential.

Fault line 04
Environmental Cost
Concept 12
Accelerating, underreported
Common misread

"AI is a digital tool — using it has no physical footprint the way flying or printing does."

Reframe

Inference drives 80–90% of AI's energy use. 945 TWh projected by 2030 (IEA). Rebound effect: efficiency gains are outpaced by consumption growth. The query running now draws on power grids that may displace community water access. Teresa Roldán, Querétaro: "Water is what's needed for the people, not for these industries."

Fault line 05
Alignment & Value Capture
Concepts 13 · 15
Whose values — unresolved
Common misread

"The model is neutral — it does what I ask. Any harm is in how I use it."

Reframe

RLHF encodes the value choices of a specific annotator pool — often low-wage workers in the Global South, filtered through the priorities of a small number of US tech companies. "Neutral" output is the product of contested, invisible labor. The model's confidence is trained in — it is not taste; it is reward-hacking.

§ 2 — Decision flows
Flow I Is this use defensible on IP grounds?
1
Will you use a commercial AI model to generate images, text, or audio?
Yes Proceed to step 2. Your output was produced by a model trained on work scraped without consent. That is a political fact regardless of current legal standing.
No Using only licensed, synthetic, or self-owned training data? Proceed — lower IP risk
2
Will the output be presented, sold, or published as your creative work?
Yes US law: AI-generated art is not copyrightable without human creative input. Document your process rigorously. Caution — disclose & document
No Tool use for process, research, or exploration. IP risk lower; ethical questions remain.
3
Does the output invoke a living artist's recognizable visual language?
Yes Style is not legally protected — but the "industrial-level identity theft" framing holds as an ethics claim even when it fails in court. Pause — reconsider
No Lower risk. Apply disclosure practices and continue.
Flow II What epistemic and cognitive risks am I taking on?
A
Am I using AI output as research, citation, or factual reference?
Yes Hallucination is structural, not incidental. The model produces plausible text — not verified facts. All factual claims require external verification. Stop — verify independently
No Using for drafts, ideation, or structure? Proceed — but the model has no understanding of what you actually intend.
B
Am I sharing unfinished, sensitive, or signature creative material with the model?
Yes Input may become training data. Glissant's right to opacity: your creative interiority doesn't owe itself to a pipeline. Consider what you authorize. Consider — what are you giving away?
No Lower cognitive exposure. Continue.
C
Am I relying on the model to make curatorial, aesthetic, or ethical judgments?
Yes The model's aesthetic confidence is trained in, not intrinsic. RLHF encodes the values of a specific annotator pool. Its confidence is reward-hacking. Pause — whose values?
No Keeping judgment in your hands. Continue.

§ 7 — AI doesn't have to be this way

The fault lines in the previous sections — extraction, hallucination, alignment failure, environmental cost — are real. They are also not permanent features of the landscape. They are the downstream consequences of specific design choices made by a small number of companies optimizing for a specific set of incentives.

Alex Komoroske, who spent 13 years inside Google and led corporate strategy at Stripe before co-founding Common Tools, makes the case plainly: the version of AI we have is not the only version that could exist. The same-origin policy that gave us data silos was a 1990s Netscape browser security decision made for reasons that had nothing to do with monopoly. It accidentally produced Facebook, Google, and every aggregator since. AI now stands at an equivalent fork. The path taken will not be chosen by default. It will be chosen by whoever is paying attention.

§ 8 — Studio practice: two bridges

The fault lines and the counterpoint above are structural — they describe what the medium does at scale, regardless of who is using it. But the medium is also being used, right now, by specific people making specific choices. Two examples from current practice, chosen because they name their tools, disclose their process, and hold their intent in view the entire time.

Both treat AI as a bridge rather than a voice. Neither treats it as a shortcut around craft. The difference between generation-for-extraction and generation-for-thought is not in the tool — it's in what the maker was already carrying before they opened it.

Abi Awomosu Writing · voice · access

"AI is not a tool. It's a medium." — something you're already swimming inside, whether or not you ever open a chatbot.

The argument

Awomosu's June 2026 essay "Writing Was Never a Test of Who Could Think" rejects the binary the discourse keeps offering — use it or refuse it — as a false choice generated by tool-thinking. A medium can't be put down. The real question is how you move through something that is already moving you.

Who the bridge is for

Her claim is specifically about access: writers who had the yin all along — the thing to say, the lived difference, the interior — but lacked the yang: time, training, a scribe, a bridge into the "straight line" of inscribed, linear writing that colonial and institutional power crowned as the only legitimate form of thought. For the web-thinker, the oral tradition-holder, the sideways mind, AI can be the bridge into legible form without demanding they first convert themselves into it. Not the voice. The bridge.

Flaw-speak
Flaw-speak, n. The deliberate degrading. The em-dash deleted, the typo inserted, the voice filed down to seem less capable than it is — writing worse than you normally would in order to avoid being accused of writing too well. The first "-speak" in the internet's history that runs backward: every prior form (txt-speak, SEO-speak, algo-speak) stretched to be more legible to whatever controlled access. Flaw-speak is the only one built on shrinking yourself.
Practice

Disclose AI's involvement upfront, without apology. Develop the ear before the mouth — the medium's dopamine architecture pushes toward constant generation; the discipline is receptivity first. Write toward lived difference rather than the flattened center RLHF trains all models toward. Refuse flaw-speak — do not perform incompetence to survive detection culture.

Dimitry Saïd Chamy Code · installation · return

A practice doesn't die. It goes dormant. The bridge back in isn't always a new skill — sometimes it's an old one, resuscitated.

The throughline

Coding began as a teenager and continued through an MFA — HTML front-end work, Macromedia Director, Flash and ActionScript, visual toys, laptop performances. It became a living: design, branding, client websites, which pulled the practice toward databases, taxonomies, and information architecture — a thread that still runs through the work as artist, educator, and cultural producer, including a continued commitment to the IndieWeb, the open, decentralized web from before platform capitalism closed it in, now being reclaimed by a new generation reacting to the same extractive forces named throughout this broadside.

Then the practice went dormant for over a decade. Not abandoned — just unreachable, the fluency gone soft, the syntax rusted.

The Unreading Room

An app collecting literary quotes — thinkers, writers, activists, theoreticians — organized around a single throughline: expression as freedom. Ideas that build the capacity to think independently and agentically. Pro-library, pro-literature, pro-free-speech, anti-censorship, anti-thought-control. The interface is deliberately simple: one screen, one randomized quote, always sourced, with a direct link to the author's page on Bookshop.org so readers can support the author and buy the book. No algorithm optimizing for engagement. No infinite scroll. One idea at a time, then the choice to go deeper or move on.

The bridge back

Initial build in ChatGPT — this was before working with Claude. Since then, the code has been substantially improved, with a better version in progress and an independent database being developed to move off dependency on any single platform's data structure. Vibe coding didn't teach a new practice; it reopened an old one, letting fifteen years of dormant fluency and a decade of information-architecture instinct meet a bridge that could hold the syntax while the underlying judgment — what the piece needed to say, how it needed to feel — stayed entirely the maker's own.

Where it's going: AR

The next phase projects the quotes into augmented reality — externalized into the physical world rather than contained on a screen. The intent is to make the literature live in the world, not just live in an app: readers encounter a quote in AR wherever they choose to place it, capture it, share it, and — the actual goal beneath the interaction — read more and act on what they've learned. The screen becomes a portal rather than a destination.

Five-step practice model
01
Disclose the medium
State plainly that AI was involved. No apology, no hedging. Disclosure is the precondition for trust, not an admission of diminished authorship.
02
Bridge, not voice
The model carries you into legible form or back into fluent syntax. It does not decide what you have to say or what the work needs to mean.
03
Ear before mouth
The medium's architecture pushes toward constant generation. The discipline is receptivity first — listening for what's actually alive before producing more.
04
Toward lived difference
Write and build toward the specific, the sideways, the un-averaged — resisting the flattened center that RLHF trains every model toward by default.
05
Refuse both failure modes
Neither slop (all mouth, no ear, generate-and-paste) nor flaw-speak (deliberately degrading your own capability to evade detection). Both are the medium winning.
§ 9 — Reference: the empirical case
Study Finding Relevance
Liang et al., Patterns (Cell Press), 2023 GPT detectors carry a 61.3% false-positive rate on non-native English essays vs. 5.1% on native — the tool did not invent the bias, it scaled it. Names who flaw-speak protects and who detection culture punishes first
Doshi & Hauser, Science Advances, 2024 AI-assisted stories were 10.7% more similar to each other. Individual gain, collective flattening. The homogenization risk Awomosu's "lived difference" directly resists
Agarwal, Naaman & Vashistha, ACM CHI, 2025 AI suggestions pushed non-Western writers toward Western styles, altering both what and how they wrote. First peer-reviewed evidence of AI-driven cultural stereotyping in writing
Atari, Xue, Park, Blasi & Henrich, Harvard, 2023 GPT's psychological profile clusters with Western liberal nations (between Germany and New Zealand) on World Values Survey data. Alignment weakens with cultural distance from the US. The empirical ZIP code behind "the Empire average" — why bridging outward requires deliberate resistance
Padmakumar & He, ICLR, 2024 Writing with feedback-tuned (RLHF) models reduced lexical and content diversity, increasing similarity between different authors. Traces homogenization to the specific mechanism: RLHF, not the base model
Shumailov et al., Nature, 2024 Model collapse: training on recursively generated synthetic data erodes the tails of the distribution over successive generations. The technical mechanism behind why an averaging medium degrades without difference fed back in
Draxler et al., ACM TOCHI, 2024 Measurable gap between felt ownership and self-declared authorship when AI produces text — "the AI ghostwriter effect." Names the dissociation Awomosu describes: pride replaced by feeling like a thief
Fan et al., British Journal of Educational Technology, 2025 ChatGPT improved essay scores but reduced metacognitive processes — evaluation, monitoring, reflection. The ear going quiet while the mouth keeps moving — the risk the five-step model is built to prevent
